# How to get a contractor license in the US

There is no national contractor license in the US. Each state decides which work needs a license and what it takes to get one, and cities and counties can add their own rules. The steps below are the ones most states share; the examples come from the boards of California and Texas, so you can see what the rules look like before you read your own state's.

## Find your state's board

Search for your state's name and "contractor license board". The board is a state agency, and its site ends in `.gov`: in California it is the Contractors State License Board (CSLB); in Texas, electricians are licensed by the Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation (TDLR). Read the rules on the board's own site, not on a site that sells exam courses.

## Check whether your work needs a license

States draw the line in different places: by the size of the job, by the trade, or by whether the job needs a permit.

- **California:** a CSLB license is required when the project needs a building permit, when you use any workers, or when the labor and materials of the project add up to $1,000 or more.
- **Texas:** anyone who does electrical work must be licensed, whatever the size of the job.

Ask the building department of the city or county where you work as well: it may ask for its own registration before it issues a permit.

## Document your experience

Boards ask for proof that you know the trade. In California you need at least four years of experience in the classification you apply for, and each claim must be confirmed by someone who saw your work firsthand: an employer, a foreman, another journeyman, a building inspector or a homeowner, among others.

Keep a record of every job from the start: the dates, the work you did and who you did it for. It is much harder to rebuild years later.

## Pass the exams

Many states test two things: the trade itself, and the law and business of running a contracting company. In California you take both, a Law and Business exam and the exam of your trade, unless you qualify for a waiver.

The CSLB offers the Law and Business exam and nine trade exams in Spanish: General Building, Concrete, Drywall, Flooring, Landscaping, Painting, Plumbing, Roofing and Tile. Before you study, ask your board which exams it offers in your language.

## Get the bond

Before the license is issued, the board may ask for a bond. California asks for a $25,000 contractor bond, or a $25,000 cashier's check filed with the CSLB; it protects clients harmed by defective work or a license law violation, and workers who were not paid. Ask your board what it asks for, including whether you need workers' compensation insurance for your employees.

## Run the business

In California the license is for someone 18 or older who can run the daily work of a construction business, including supervising the job site. Once you have the license, put its number on your quotes and contracts: clients can look it up on the board's site.

## Working in another state

A license is valid in the state that issued it. To work across the state line, ask that state's board what it requires before you bid.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. The rules change; check them with your state's board before you apply. Once you're licensed, the calculators below work out the materials for each quote.
