# Drywall tools

Count what a room takes before you order: sheets of 4 × 8 or 4 × 12 ft, joint compound and joint tape, with the doors and windows left out and the extra for cuts you choose. The calculator also totals the cost of the sheets when you enter your price.

The guide goes further: the levels of finish of the Gypsum Association, the biggest labor difference between two quotes, and how to write a quote your client can check.

## Plan a drywall job in four steps

1. **Measure the room.** Take the length and the width of the floor and the height of the walls, and count the doors and the windows: the [drywall calculator](/drywall-calculator) takes them off. The wall area is the distance around the room times the height, and the ceiling is the length times the width.
2. **Choose the sheet.** A 4 × 8 ft sheet covers 32 sq ft and a 4 × 12 ft sheet 48 sq ft. The longer sheet leaves fewer joints to tape on a long wall, but it is heavier and needs two people. The room pages give both counts, for example the [drywall for a 12 × 12 ft room](/drywall/drywall-for-12x12-room).
3. **Count the finishing.** USG puts its all-purpose joint compound at about two 4.5-gallon pails for 1,000 sq ft, and its paper tape at about 370 ft for 1,000 sq ft. The calculator works both out from those figures and lists the [sources](/drywall-calculator#sources).
4. **Agree on the level of finish.** The Gypsum Association's GA-214 sets six levels, from 0 to 5, and each one adds coats of compound. It is the biggest labor difference between two quotes for the same room, so write it in the quote.

Hang the ceiling before the walls, keep the extra for cuts around openings and corners, and check what your local code asks for in a bathroom or on a ceiling. [How to estimate a drywall job](/guides/how-to-estimate-a-drywall-job) goes through a whole room.
