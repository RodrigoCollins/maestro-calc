<h2 id="how-to-use">How to use the drywall calculator</h2>

The calculator tells you how many sheets of drywall a room takes, and how much joint compound and tape you need to finish them. The result updates as you type, so there is nothing to submit.

1. Measure the length and the width of the room at the floor, and the height of the walls. Type them the way you read a tape: 12 ft, 12 ft 6 in or 8 ft 1 1/2 in. A plain number is read in feet.
2. Count the doors and the windows. The calculator leaves out about 20 square feet for each door and about 15 square feet for each window.
3. Choose what you are covering: the walls and the ceiling, or the walls only.
4. Choose the sheet size: 4 × 8 ft or 4 × 12 ft.
5. Keep the 10 percent extra for cuts and breakage, or change it.
6. To see the cost, type the price per sheet.

The result leads with the area to cover. It also shows the same area in square meters, the gallons of joint compound, the feet of joint tape, and the shopping list: the sheets, the 4.5-gallon pails of compound and the 500 ft rolls of tape.

To work in metric, press Metric (m, cm). Lengths are then read in meters, and the result leads with square meters. Switching units never changes your numbers, only how they are written. <h2 id="formulas">Formulas and worked example</h2>

The calculator works from the size of the room to what you buy:

- Wall area = 2 × (length + width) × height, less 20 square feet per door and 15 square feet per window, never below zero.
- Area to cover = wall area, plus length × width for the ceiling when you include it.
- Sheets = area × (1 + extra ÷ 100) ÷ the area of one sheet (32 or 48 square feet), rounded up.
- Joint compound = area × 9 gallons per 1,000 square feet; pails = gallons ÷ 4.5, rounded up.
- Joint tape = area × 370 feet per 1,000 square feet; rolls = feet ÷ 500, rounded up.

The compound rate comes from USG, which says about two 4.5-gallon pails of its lightweight all-purpose compound finish 1,000 square feet. The tape rate is the coverage on the USG data sheet for its paper joint tape. Both are approximately right for a standard finish; a smoother finish takes more compound.

Worked example: a bedroom 12 ft by 12 ft with 8 ft walls, one door, two windows, walls and ceiling, 4 × 8 sheets and 10 percent extra.

- The walls are 2 × (12 + 12) × 8 = 384 square feet. One door and two windows leave out 50, so 334 remain.
- The ceiling adds 12 × 12 = 144, so the area to cover is 478.00 square feet, or 44.41 square meters.
- With 10 percent extra that is 525.8 square feet, and 525.8 ÷ 32 = 16.4, so you buy 17 sheets.
- Joint compound: 478 × 9 ÷ 1,000 = 4.30 gallons, so 1 pail.
- Joint tape: 478 × 370 ÷ 1,000 = 176.86 feet, so 1 roll.
- At $12 a sheet, the sheets cost $204.00.

The calculator keeps exact fractions from the first input to the last step. The purchase counts are rounded up, and every other figure is rounded only when it is shown. The page [How we calculate](/methodology#drywall) lists the formulas and the cases we test.

<h2 id="tips">Tips and common mistakes</h2>

Use 4 × 12 sheets on long walls when you can carry them. Fewer sheets mean fewer joints to tape and finish. In the example room, 4 × 12 sheets bring the count down from 17 to 11.

Hang the ceiling first, then the walls. The wall sheets then hold up the edges of the ceiling sheets.

Keep the extra for cuts. Around doors, windows and outlets you cut a lot of pieces you cannot reuse. Raise the extra for a room with many corners, a sloped ceiling or a stairwell.

The calculator does not count screws, corner bead or trim. Count the outside corners and the length of trim on site, and ask your supplier how many screws fit your framing and the code where you build.

Ceilings and wet areas often need special panels, such as sag-resistant ceiling board or moisture-resistant board for a bathroom. Check what your local code requires.

These are estimates. Confirm the quantities with your supplier before you order.

<h2 id="faq">Frequently asked questions</h2>

### How many sheets of drywall do I need?

Add up the area of the walls and the ceiling, take out the doors and windows, add about 10 percent for cuts and divide by the area of one sheet. A 12 ft by 12 ft room with 8 ft walls, one door and two windows needs about 17 sheets of 4 × 8 for walls and ceiling.

### How many square feet is a sheet of drywall?

A 4 × 8 ft sheet covers 32 square feet and a 4 × 12 ft sheet covers 48 square feet. Thickness does not change the area.

### How much joint compound do I need?

About 9 gallons per 1,000 square feet of drywall, based on USG's figure of two 4.5-gallon pails of lightweight all-purpose compound for 1,000 square feet. The example room takes about 4.30 gallons, so 1 pail.

### How much drywall tape do I need?

About 370 feet of paper tape per 1,000 square feet, the coverage USG gives for its joint tape. The example room takes about 176.86 feet, so one 500 ft roll.

### Should I include the ceiling?

Include it when you are hanging new drywall on the ceiling too. Choose walls only when the ceiling stays as it is. In the example, the ceiling adds 144 square feet and 5 sheets.

### How much extra drywall should I buy?

The calculator starts at 10 percent for cuts and breakage. Use more for rooms with many corners or angles. Without any extra, the example room would need 15 sheets instead of 17.

### Does the calculator count screws?

No. The number of screws depends on the framing spacing, the panel orientation and the code where you build. Ask your supplier or follow the panel maker's fastening schedule.

### Can I use meters?

Yes. Press Metric (m, cm), then type the length, the width and the wall height in meters. The result leads with square meters, and the sheets are still 4 × 8 or 4 × 12 ft. Switching units never changes your numbers, only how they are written.
